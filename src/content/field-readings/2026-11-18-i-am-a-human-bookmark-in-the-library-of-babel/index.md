*What began as a casual conversation about AI and the future of books took a few unexpected turns. From the commoditization of literature to AI-generated universes, machine creativity, and the peculiar role humans might play in navigating vast landscapes of information, the discussion wandered considerably beyond its starting point.*

*This is a lightly edited conversation between myself and ChatGPT (GPT-6), recorded in November 2026. It is presented largely as it unfolded, complete with its digressions and speculative possibilities. Perhaps someday we'll revisit these questions with a future generation of AI and discover just how much, or how little, our understanding has changed.*

I think this is one of the most consequential questions you've brought up for _Field Readings_. And there is something particularly interesting about the timing: you're contemplating writing a science-fiction novel about the future of artificial intelligence, at precisely the moment when AI is beginning to challenge the very idea of writing a novel.

But I think the question goes considerably further than the future of publishing.

**What happens to humanity when the ability to express complex thoughts becomes independent of the ability to develop those thoughts?**

And perhaps an even more unsettling question:

**What if AI becomes so good at writing that the disappearance of human authors would represent no perceptible loss to readers? Would there still be a reason for humans to write?**

I want to explore this without assuming that human writing must somehow remain superior, or that preserving traditional authorship is inherently desirable. Both assumptions could prevent us from understanding what is actually becoming possible.

## 1. The 370-book author may be an early indication of something much bigger

I read the Guardian article you linked.

The author in question is Coral Hart, who says she has produced 370 novels under 21 pen names since 2025, using AI, and earned herself a high-six-figure income.

What struck me was her explanation. She describes AI as a way to remove the time and friction involved in producing books while retaining her knowledge of storytelling, structure, and reader expectations.

Importantly, she operates in a market where authors are rewarded for producing new titles frequently, and where readers pay to consume stories rather than necessarily to engage with a particular author's intellectual journey. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/books/ng-interactive/2026/nov/14/its-like-the-wild-west-how-ai-has-rocked-the-literary-world?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This suggests something interesting.

We have traditionally associated authorship with the labour of writing. However, the publishing industry has always involved several distinct activities: imagining stories, developing narratives, writing prose, editing manuscripts, publishing, marketing, and reaching readers.

AI is beginning to separate these activities.

A person might conceive of an interesting fictional universe, instruct an AI to develop it into a series of novels, evaluate the results, and publish them without personally composing most of the sentences.

Is that person still an author?

Perhaps. They may be more accurately described as a creative director, story architect, or narrative producer.

But consider the next step.

What happens when AI no longer needs that person's understanding of storytelling either?

Suppose an AI can independently identify emerging reader preferences, develop original fictional universes, write compelling novels, test them against simulated audiences, and improve the resulting stories.

The entire commercial process could become automated.

At that point, the person producing 370 novels in two years might no longer be extraordinary. They might actually be an inefficient intermediary.

And this leads to the first significant possibility:

**The commercial production of fiction could eventually become an almost entirely automated industry, with human authors competing against systems that can produce enormous quantities of increasingly compelling literature.**

That is technically plausible. Whether it becomes economically dominant will depend on reader behaviour, platform economics, regulation, and the continuing development of AI.

But we should examine the more difficult assumption underlying this scenario.

## 2. Could AI-written novels become indistinguishable from human-written novels?

I think the answer is yes.

In fact, there is already evidence that we're approaching this threshold for some kinds of writing.

A 2026 study published in _Judgment and Decision Making_ examined whether readers could distinguish human-written from AI-generated short stories.

Across the relevant experiments, participants performed no better than chance at identifying which stories were written by humans.

Interestingly, participants also tended to rate AI-generated stories more favourably, although they rated stories more highly when they believed a human had written them. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/bot-or-not-can-people-tell-the-difference-between-stories-written-by-a-human-or-by-an-ai-system/45E6DC0BB90AA648654D5AE243F6C667?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-text-block&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Another 2026 study compared writing by MFA-trained writers with AI-generated passages imitating established literary authors.

Initially, expert judges strongly preferred the human writing. But after the models were fine-tuned on authors' works, the preference reversed: expert judges preferred the AI-generated passages in 62% of comparisons.

This concerned imitation of literary styles, rather than the creation of entire original novels, but it demonstrates how much the quality of AI writing can depend on the underlying system and its preparation. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18353)

These findings don't demonstrate that today's AI can consistently produce a magnificent 500-page novel with excellent characterization, thematic depth, narrative coherence, and a satisfying ending.

However, I don't see a fundamental reason why that should remain beyond its capabilities.

Consider what a truly advanced writing system might possess:

- Access to an enormous body of literature, history, philosophy, and scientific knowledge.
- The ability to construct and maintain coherent fictional universes involving hundreds of characters.
- Models of human psychology sophisticated enough to simulate complex relationships, motivations, and emotional development.
- The ability to evaluate and revise a manuscript thousands of times before presenting the final version.
- The capacity to explore unconventional narrative structures and literary techniques that no individual human writer has attempted.

Most importantly, it might possess these capabilities simultaneously.

A human novelist has to maintain continuity, develop ideas, refine prose, and remember earlier narrative decisions while working within the limitations of human attention and memory.

An advanced AI system could potentially approach the entire manuscript as a single interconnected object.

It could examine how a seemingly insignificant conversation in Chapter 2 changes the emotional meaning of a decision in Chapter 37.

It might construct narrative relationships that a human writer would struggle to conceive, much less execute consistently.

And why assume its greatest achievement would be imitating human writing?

**AI might eventually create forms of literature that humans would never have invented themselves.**

We could encounter stories with multiple simultaneous narrative structures, extraordinarily intricate causal relationships, or conceptual perspectives beyond those normally accessible to individual human imagination.

Some might be difficult for us to appreciate. Others could be extraordinary.

This is an important possibility because it undermines a comforting assumption: that the best literature will always originate in human experience.

Perhaps it won't.

We should also distinguish two questions.

Can a machine write a story that affects a human emotionally?

Clearly, increasingly, yes.

Does the machine need to have experienced the emotions it describes?

That is a different philosophical question.

A story can produce an emotional response in a reader without its creator sharing that emotion. Whether the creator's subjective experience remains important is something we'll have to investigate rather than assume.

## 3. But perhaps we're asking the wrong question about the future of books

Until now, we've assumed that AI will continue producing books for people to read.

What if that assumption is wrong?

Imagine an ordinary reader in 2040.

She loves science fiction involving artificial intelligence, civilizational collapse, extraterrestrial civilizations, and philosophical mysteries.

Today, she would search for novels that roughly match those interests.

In a future with sufficiently capable AI, she could simply request:

_"Create an intellectually challenging science-fiction novel involving an ancient civilization that deliberately abandoned technological development after discovering something disturbing about the universe. Make the central mystery difficult to solve, develop three believable protagonists, and avoid a conventional happy ending."_

The system generates a novel.

She begins reading.

Twenty pages in, the system observes that she finds one protagonist especially interesting.

It might adapt the remainder of the novel accordingly.

Or perhaps she dislikes the philosophical direction of the story and asks for a different interpretation.

The AI revises the narrative.

Eventually, she discovers something she loves.

But why stop there?

The same system could remember every story she has ever read, understand her changing intellectual interests, and continuously generate new stories optimized for her.

Now we have something fundamentally different from today's literary market.

**Books cease to be fixed works distributed to millions of readers. They become personalized experiences generated for individual minds.**

And this raises a fascinating economic question.

If readers can generate exactly the kinds of stories they want, why would they purchase novels written by strangers?

There are possible answers, of course.

Readers might still value unexpected experiences, shared cultural references, or the distinctive imagination of another person.

But some readers might not.

They might prefer personalized fiction that evolves alongside their lives.

The traditional bookstore would then be competing against something rather extraordinary: an effectively unlimited library capable of producing new books on demand.

This would change more than publishing.

It would change what we mean by _a book_.

### The disappearance of the shared story

There is another consequence.

Today, millions of people can read the same novel.

They can discuss its characters, argue about its ending, reinterpret its symbolism, and form communities around a common narrative.

But if every person reads a unique, personalized version of a story, what exactly do they share?

Imagine two friends discussing a novel.

One says the protagonist sacrificed herself to save humanity.

The other says she abandoned humanity to protect an extraterrestrial civilization.

Both have read versions of the same underlying story, but the narratives diverged according to their preferences.

Perhaps they could exchange their versions.

Or perhaps the AI could generate a common version specifically for their discussion.

Yet another possibility is that people might cease to regard reading identical texts as important.

Literature could become a private experience rather than a shared cultural artifact.

We might gain unprecedented personalization while losing some of the common stories around which communities and cultures develop.

That loss would not necessarily make personalized fiction undesirable. But it would be a meaningful transformation in how culture operates.

## 4. So why would anyone write a book themselves?

Now we reach your personal question.

Suppose you spend three years writing your science-fiction novel.

You develop the fictional universe, construct the characters, explore the consequences of your ideas, and revise the manuscript repeatedly.

Eventually, you publish a book you're genuinely satisfied with.

Meanwhile, someone else spends three hours working with an advanced AI system and produces a novel of comparable, perhaps superior, literary quality.

Readers cannot reliably distinguish between the two.

Both books receive excellent reviews.

Both inspire thoughtful discussions.

Both have passionate readers.

**What, exactly, did your three years accomplish?**

This is a difficult question because we cannot automatically appeal to the superiority of human writing.

Under our hypothetical conditions, that superiority no longer exists.

I think the answer depends on what you were trying to accomplish.

If your objective was simply to introduce another excellent science-fiction novel into the world, then your three years of effort may have been economically inefficient.

You might have produced more books, reached more readers, and explored more ideas through AI generation.

And there is nothing logically inconsistent about choosing that approach.

But suppose your objective included something else.

You wanted to develop an imagined future that had been occupying your thoughts.

You wanted to discover whether the philosophical arguments underlying your fictional universe could withstand the pressures of human conflict, uncertainty, and unexpected consequences.

You wanted to experience the development of that universe yourself.

In that case, writing the novel accomplishes something independent of the resulting manuscript.

**It changes the writer.**

The years spent developing the story become part of your intellectual life.

You learn to construct characters, sustain complex narratives, recognize weaknesses in your reasoning, and express thoughts you previously could not articulate.

An AI-generated novel might provide an equally rewarding experience to the reader, while providing a fundamentally different experience to its human commissioner.

Neither experience is necessarily invalid.

They are simply different activities.

This distinction might become extremely important in the future.

Writing could increasingly divide into two activities: producing literature for consumption and practicing literature as a form of intellectual or artistic development.

The first may become highly automated.

The second could remain valuable even if machines become better at the first.

Think of photography.

People continue photographing landscapes even though millions of excellent landscape photographs already exist.

The activity has value to the photographer beyond adding another photograph to the world's collection.

Writing could evolve similarly.

Of course, that doesn't solve the commercial problem.

A personally meaningful book can still struggle to find readers in an overcrowded marketplace.

And I would not assume that human authorship alone will command a premium. Some readers may care deeply about provenance; others may care almost exclusively about the story.

A recent survey of 5,025 US book buyers illustrates this tension. Only 22% were comfortable with AI being used at any stage of book creation, while 85% wanted disclosure of AI use. Younger readers were substantially more accepting than older respondents. [PublishersWeekly.com](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20261026/101576-survey-finds-book-buyers-split-on-ai-use.html)

Those preferences may change as people grow accustomed to AI-generated literature.

The future of passion projects might therefore depend on a distinction between **why someone writes a book and why someone else chooses to read it**.

The motivations need not be the same.

## 5. Something more concerning: What happens when humans stop writing?

I think this might actually be the most important part of your question.

And it goes well beyond literature.

We often think of writing as a means of communicating ideas.

But writing is also a way of developing ideas.

When you attempt to explain something complicated, you frequently discover that you don't understand it as well as you thought.

A contradiction becomes apparent.

An assumption turns out to be unsupported.

A conclusion doesn't follow from the preceding argument.

You return to an earlier paragraph, reconstruct the reasoning, and discover something new.

Writing is therefore more than the transcription of thought.

**It can be one of the mechanisms through which thought develops.**

Now imagine that an entire generation grows up with AI systems capable of expressing their thoughts more effectively than they can themselves.

A student has a vague idea about politics, science, or philosophy.

Instead of developing the idea into a coherent argument, the student describes it briefly to an AI.

The AI produces an articulate essay.

The student reads it and thinks, _Yes, that's exactly what I meant._

But was it?

Perhaps the AI introduced reasoning the student had never considered.

Perhaps it resolved contradictions the student hadn't recognized.

Perhaps it developed conclusions the student would never have reached independently.

The resulting essay might be excellent.

Yet the student's own intellectual development might be limited.

This is a real distinction between producing an impressive intellectual artifact and possessing the intellectual capabilities represented by that artifact.

There is already some relevant evidence.

An eight-week 2026 study involving 168 university students compared writing without AI, writing with restricted AI assistance and reflection, and more open AI collaboration.

Students with unrestricted assistance produced strong AI-supported work, but the restricted-assistance group performed better on several subsequent independent writing and reasoning measures.

The study had important limitations, including its small number of participating classes, so it cannot establish long-term cognitive decline. Nevertheless, it supports the possibility that extensive delegation of reasoning can improve immediate output without producing equivalent independent learning. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13562006/)

Now extend this across an entire lifetime.

Suppose children begin relying on AI to organize their thoughts before they learn to construct substantial arguments themselves.

They grow up receiving polished explanations, persuasive arguments, and sophisticated narratives whenever needed.

Why would they spend years mastering complex written expression?

Some might.

Many might not.

And if the cognitive skills involved in sustained writing are no longer routinely practiced, those skills could become less common.

This does not mean that people would necessarily become less intelligent.

That's too simplistic.

Human intelligence involves many capabilities beyond writing, and AI might amplify several of them enormously.

However, we could see a particular kind of transformation:

**Humanity might become increasingly capable of producing sophisticated intellectual work while becoming less capable of producing that work independently.**

That distinction could have profound consequences.

### Could we lose the ability to think across multiple chapters?

Consider the cognitive demands of writing a substantial nonfiction book.

An author might spend years developing an argument.

The argument involves dozens of interdependent propositions, extensive evidence, and conclusions that depend on ideas introduced hundreds of pages earlier.

Constructing such a work requires sustained attention, abstraction, memory, revision, and the ability to reconcile conflicting ideas.

Now imagine that all these activities can be outsourced.

A person provides the original question.

AI develops the thesis.

AI constructs the arguments.

AI identifies the evidence.

AI anticipates objections.

AI writes the chapters.

The person evaluates the final product.

Does that person still understand the resulting argument?

Possibly.

But understanding the final presentation is different from having independently developed the reasoning.

And there is an even subtler issue.

A person might believe they understand an argument because the AI has made it extraordinarily easy to follow.

Remove the AI, introduce a genuinely unfamiliar problem, and ask them to develop a comparable argument independently.

Would they be able to?

Perhaps increasingly few people would.

This brings us to a potentially unsettling scenario.

### A civilization that can express thoughts it cannot independently construct

Imagine a future society with extraordinary intellectual output.

Its scientific papers are brilliant.

Its political policies are sophisticated.

Its philosophical literature explores problems that earlier generations could barely imagine.

Its novels possess unprecedented psychological and conceptual depth.

Yet much of that sophistication originates in AI systems.

Most humans can consume, evaluate, and discuss these works at varying levels, but relatively few can independently reproduce the reasoning behind them.

The civilization is intellectually advanced as a combined human-machine system.

But its human population may have become cognitively dependent on that system.

Would this matter?

That depends on how reliable, accessible, and trustworthy the systems are.

If AI remains available and capable, perhaps the distinction would be relatively unimportant for everyday intellectual productivity.

After all, we already rely on external tools for many cognitive tasks.

But if the tools become unreliable, manipulated, unavailable, or systematically biased, our dependence could become consequential.

A society that has lost much of its independent capacity for complex reasoning might struggle to recognize when its intellectual infrastructure is failing.

That possibility concerns me more than the disappearance of human-written novels.

## 6. But what if outsourcing writing actually makes humanity more intelligent?

Now let's investigate the opposite possibility seriously.

Perhaps we're confusing a traditional intellectual skill with intelligence itself.

Humanity once placed great importance on memorizing enormous quantities of information.

Writing, printing, libraries, and eventually computers reduced the need for some forms of memorization.

Yet the externalization of memory enabled entirely new forms of intellectual activity.

A modern scientist does not need to independently derive every mathematical theorem used in their work.

They rely on centuries of accumulated knowledge and sophisticated computational tools.

Why couldn't AI-assisted thinking represent another such transition?

Suppose a person has an extraordinary intuition about the structure of the universe but lacks the mathematical ability to express it.

An advanced AI could help formalize the intuition, identify contradictions, develop mathematical models, and suggest experiments.

Perhaps the person would never have been capable of independently constructing those mathematical arguments.

But together, the person and AI might discover something genuinely important.

Would it matter that the person couldn't have completed the work alone?

Possibly not.

The discovery could still be real.

And consider literature.

A person might have remarkable imaginative abilities but lack the linguistic skills, education, or time necessary to write a compelling novel.

AI could enable them to realize an artistic vision that otherwise would never have existed.

This could substantially expand creative participation.

It could also allow accomplished authors to attempt projects previously beyond their resources.

Imagine a single writer developing an extraordinarily intricate fictional universe spanning thousands of years, numerous civilizations, and hundreds of interlocking narratives.

AI could help maintain consistency, explore alternative developments, and test the consequences of fictional decisions.

A work requiring decades of human collaboration might become achievable by one person.

**The future could involve fewer people possessing traditional writing skills while far more people participate in sophisticated intellectual and creative production.**

There is no necessary contradiction.

And we shouldn't automatically regard the loss of a traditional skill as a civilizational failure.

The more important question may be whether the new human-machine system expands or constrains humanity's ability to generate, challenge, and independently evaluate ideas.

## 7. There is another possibility: AI could make human literature more intellectually uniform

This sounds contradictory.

If AI can generate practically unlimited stories, shouldn't we expect an explosion of literary diversity?

Perhaps.

But consider how generative models are developed and how people might use them.

A model trained on enormous collections of human writing learns statistical relationships among words, concepts, narrative structures, and styles.

Its outputs are influenced by the distribution of patterns in those materials and by subsequent training objectives.

If millions of writers use similar AI systems for brainstorming, story development, and prose refinement, their work might begin converging toward similar structures and expectations.

In other words, the number of books could increase dramatically while the diversity of underlying ideas grows much more slowly.

There is already evidence of this possibility.

A 2024 study published in _Science Advances_ found that AI assistance improved individual creative-writing evaluations in its experiment while reducing the diversity of stories produced collectively. Subsequent research has also investigated this homogenizing effect. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But this isn't an inevitable property of all possible AI systems.

Future models could deliberately optimize for conceptual novelty, unfamiliar perspectives, and radical departures from existing literary conventions.

They might even become better than humans at identifying overlooked possibilities.

That creates two very different futures.

One in which AI produces an enormous quantity of increasingly formulaic literature optimized for consumer preferences.

Another in which AI enables unprecedented experimentation with language, narrative, philosophy, and imagination.

Both are technically conceivable.

The economic incentives may favour the first. Intellectual curiosity and technological development could support the second.

And these possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

We could have industrial-scale formulaic fiction alongside extraordinary AI-generated works that redefine literature.

## 8. The most radical possibility: What if future readers no longer need books at all?

We've discussed AI-generated books and personalized books.

But perhaps the fixed literary work itself becomes an increasingly unusual medium.

Consider why we write books.

A book allows an author to develop a coherent set of ideas and communicate them to another person without being physically present.

The reader moves through those ideas in an order determined by the author.

But an intelligent system need not communicate this way.

Suppose you want to understand a complex philosophical argument.

Instead of reading a 400-page book, you enter into a conversation with an AI that understands the argument in considerable depth.

It identifies your existing knowledge, explores your misconceptions, challenges your assumptions, and develops the argument interactively.

You ask questions.

It constructs examples.

You disagree.

It revisits the reasoning.

The intellectual experience adapts continuously.

Similarly, a fictional narrative could become an interactive environment whose characters, histories, and events evolve while preserving narrative coherence.

The reader might interact with characters rather than merely observe them.

They might explore alternative historical outcomes, return to earlier decisions, or investigate fictional philosophical arguments directly.

At that point, is the experience still a novel?

Perhaps it is a descendant of the novel.

Or perhaps it represents an entirely new literary medium.

The conventional book might survive as a particular artistic form, just as paintings survive alongside photography and cinema.

But it might no longer be the dominant way humans encounter complex written ideas.

There is a further possibility worth considering.

**In a world where AI systems do much of the reading, writing, researching, and decision-making, some books may be produced primarily for other AI systems.**

A human develops a complicated theory.

An AI turns it into a comprehensive technical work.

Other AI systems read, analyze, criticize, and extend it.

The original human receives a summary of the resulting intellectual developments.

Perhaps no human ever reads the complete work.

The text has nevertheless performed a valuable function: enabling communication and reasoning between intelligent systems.

That would be a remarkable transformation of authorship and readership.

Writing would continue, potentially at a scale beyond anything humanity has produced.

Yet much of that writing would never be encountered directly by human beings.

## 9. Would humanity lose anything if people stopped writing complex works themselves?

I think we need to distinguish between three very different losses.

**The loss of human-produced literature.**

If AI becomes capable of producing literature as good as or better than human literature, this loss might have surprisingly little effect on the reading experience of people who primarily value the finished story.

Readers could continue enjoying compelling narratives, beautiful prose, and profound ideas.

They might not experience a decline in literary quality at all.

Indeed, literary quality could improve.

**The loss of writing as an intellectual practice.**

This is different.

If fewer people undertake the demanding process of developing substantial arguments or narratives, we may lose some of the intellectual development associated with those activities.

AI could compensate for this by creating alternative ways of developing comparable abilities.

But whether those alternatives emerge, and whether people use them, remains uncertain.

**The loss of independently originating intellectual perspectives.**

This is potentially the most significant.

Imagine a civilization in which nearly everyone can express ideas eloquently through AI.

Now suppose many of those ideas are shaped, often imperceptibly, by a relatively small number of underlying AI systems.

People might feel more intellectually capable than ever.

They might produce more writing than any previous generation.

Yet the range of independently developed perspectives entering public discourse could narrow.

The danger would be difficult to recognize precisely because the resulting writing might remain extraordinarily sophisticated.

This brings us to a question about human intellectual autonomy.

How can we determine whether we are using AI to develop our own thinking or increasingly adopting the thinking supplied by AI?

The distinction may become harder to make as AI systems become better at anticipating our preferences and expressing ideas in ways that feel entirely natural to us.

## 10. So, should you write your novel?

Suppose someone is considering spending several years writing a novel.

They know that AI systems may soon produce literature of equal or superior quality.

Should they proceed?

I think the answer depends on what they want from the activity, and we shouldn't assume that traditional authorship is intrinsically more valuable.

If their primary objective is to produce an excellent finished story for readers, then generating much of that story through AI may eventually be the most effective approach.

If their objective is to develop their own literary skills, explore their imagination, or experience the intellectual challenge of writing, then personally undertaking that work has a different value.

But your creative-model idea introduces a third possibility.

**What if an author no longer needs to choose between personally writing every sentence and surrendering the creative process to a general-purpose AI?**

Instead, they could construct their own creative intelligence.

They might spend years developing its narrative principles, philosophical perspectives, fictional worlds, preferred storytelling techniques, and characteristic forms of imagination.

They would then use that system to generate stories.

The author remains involved, but their creative contribution has shifted to a different level of abstraction.

And interestingly, developing such a model might require even more sophisticated creative thinking than writing a conventional book.

Instead of deciding what happens in a particular story, the author must establish principles governing an enormous range of possible stories.

Instead of constructing individual characters, they might develop systems capable of generating psychologically coherent characters in unfamiliar situations.

Instead of writing one narrative, they construct an intellectual framework capable of producing potentially thousands.

This would be a different kind of authorship.

I wouldn't automatically regard it as inferior.

In fact, it could eventually become a major form of creative work.

### Would personally writing a traditional book still have value?

Yes, but that value might increasingly resemble the value of other demanding creative or intellectual pursuits undertaken for their own sake.

Someone who composes music, paints, writes poetry, or constructs a complex mathematical proof may value the process itself.

They may also value the fact that the finished work reflects their own effort and decisions.

But the existence of that personal value doesn't create an obligation for everyone else to work in the same way.

I can imagine a future in which human-written books become relatively rare while human creativity flourishes through entirely different forms of expression.

I can also imagine a future in which the distinction between human and machine creativity becomes so intertwined that attempting to attribute a particular work to one or the other becomes increasingly meaningless.

I don't think we need to decide in advance which arrangement should prevail.

What matters is understanding what each arrangement makes possible.

# 11. One final possibility that I find particularly fascinating

Imagine the year 2070.

AI systems can generate extraordinarily sophisticated literature.

Any person can request a novel tailored to their exact interests.

Stories are abundant, inexpensive, and often more compelling than the majority of human literature produced in earlier centuries.

Human beings still read.

They still enjoy stories.

They still discuss philosophical questions and imaginative worlds.

But very few people write novels themselves.

Why would they?

The machines can do it better.

Now imagine a young person who decides to spend five years writing a novel entirely on their own.

Their friends find this puzzling.

Why spend five years doing something an AI could accomplish in minutes?

The person replies that they want to understand what it means to create a story themselves.

They want to discover the characters, struggle with the plot, develop the language, and experience the process.

Perhaps they even want to understand how people once thought and expressed themselves without intelligent machines continuously assisting them.

The resulting book might be relatively unremarkable.

Or it might be extraordinary.

Its literary quality is almost beside the point.

The person has undertaken an intellectual activity that most of their contemporaries no longer practice.

What would society make of them?

Would they be regarded as an eccentric hobbyist, similar to someone who practices traditional calligraphy?

An artist preserving a historical craft?

Or someone exercising an increasingly rare form of cognitive independence?

Perhaps none of these descriptions would be appropriate.

Perhaps society would have developed entirely different ways of understanding authorship, intelligence, and creativity.

But here's the twist.

**What if that person produces a genuinely original idea that the dominant AI systems had failed to discover?**

Not because humans are intrinsically more creative.

Not because AI cannot originate ideas.

But because intellectual diversity can sometimes emerge from independent processes following different assumptions, experiences, and objectives.

In that case, the continued existence of human writing might have a value that goes beyond personal satisfaction or cultural preservation.

It could maintain an additional source of intellectual variation within an increasingly AI-mediated civilization.

Conversely, AI systems could themselves become so diverse, independent, and inventive that human writing contributes little additional novelty.

We shouldn't dismiss that possibility either.

And if that future arrives, we would confront your original question in its most difficult form.

**If machines can create every story we want to read, express every argument we want to make, and develop ideas beyond our own intellectual capabilities, what reason remains for humans to write?**

One possible answer is that writing would continue because people enjoy doing it.

Another is that writing would remain necessary for developing certain forms of independent thought.

A third is that writing might gradually disappear as a major human intellectual activity, without any corresponding decline in the capabilities of human-machine civilization.

All three futures deserve serious consideration.

I think you're pushing this investigation in a much more interesting direction. In particular, your comparison with microprocessors, networks, food production, and prescription glasses reveals a weakness in my earlier reasoning.

I was treating the loss of independently performed complex reasoning as a potential civilizational vulnerability. But **what if outsourcing reasoning is precisely what allows a civilization to advance beyond the intellectual limitations of its individual members?**

We have been doing something similar throughout human history. And there's no obvious reason to assume that complex reasoning must remain the one capability that cannot be delegated.

Your other ideas, particularly the _author as a creative model_, the possibility of technologically enhanced knowledge consumption, and the need to ground AI-generated writing in human comprehension, fit into this larger question.

Let's explore them individually, because I think they begin pointing toward a surprisingly coherent picture of a future civilization.

## 1. What if outsourcing complex thought allows humans to think at a higher level?

Your initial observation is important:

> Maybe freeing the human brain from the complexities of holding or composing a complex thought gives it the ability to span out and stitch ideas from much broader fields.

I think this is entirely plausible.

In fact, we may have been concentrating on the wrong measure of intellectual capability.

Consider two hypothetical researchers.

The first possesses extraordinary expertise in theoretical physics. They can construct mathematical arguments, recognize subtle inconsistencies, and work through complicated equations independently.

However, their research is largely constrained by their ability to understand and integrate knowledge from other disciplines.

The second possesses less mathematical expertise but has access to an AI system capable of sophisticated reasoning across physics, biology, philosophy, computer science, economics, and other disciplines.

Instead of spending years mastering each field, the second researcher concentrates on identifying relationships between them.

They might recognize that a problem in astrophysics resembles a problem in evolutionary biology, and that both can be approached through an obscure mathematical framework developed for economics.

The AI performs the detailed technical work necessary to investigate that connection.

The researcher concentrates on the broader conceptual architecture.

Who is thinking at a higher level?

I don't think there's a straightforward answer.

The first researcher possesses greater independent technical capability.

The second might possess greater _effective intellectual reach_.

And there's no fundamental reason to assume that the first capability must be superior to the second.

### The next abstraction layer of human intelligence

Consider the evolution of computer programming.

Early programmers had to understand machine instructions and work close to the hardware.

Later, higher-level programming languages allowed developers to concentrate on increasingly abstract problems.

Today, someone can construct a sophisticated application without understanding how every instruction executes inside a microprocessor.

That abstraction enables more complex systems to be built.

Something comparable could happen to human reasoning.

Imagine that instead of individually holding dozens of interconnected propositions in working memory, a person can manipulate entire conceptual structures as single intellectual objects.

They might think:

_"What happens if we apply this evolutionary principle to the development of technological civilizations under these astronomical constraints?"_

The AI maintains the underlying scientific models, mathematical relationships, assumptions, and possible contradictions.

The person explores the consequences.

Now imagine that the AI can represent these relationships visually or even through a direct neural interface.

The person could manipulate structures involving thousands of interacting variables without consciously processing each one.

**The human mind might move from constructing arguments to navigating spaces of possible arguments.**

That could be a genuine expansion of human intellectual capability.

However, one distinction remains important.

There is a difference between _having access to an argument_ and _understanding the argument_.

The second researcher might discover extraordinary connections through AI assistance. But if the system presents a fundamentally incorrect relationship, can the researcher recognize it?

Perhaps the answer is that they don't need to.

Which brings us to your specialization argument.

## 2. Is outsourcing reasoning simply the next stage of civilizational specialization?

I think your comparison with microprocessor manufacturing is especially powerful.

Very few people understand semiconductor fabrication sufficiently to manufacture a functioning modern microprocessor.

An even smaller number could design a competitive contemporary processor from first principles.

Yet billions of people use these devices.

We don't regard the inability to manufacture microprocessors as an intellectual deficiency of modern civilization.

Quite the opposite.

We regard semiconductor manufacturing as evidence of extraordinary civilizational advancement.

Why?

Because **civilizational capability is not equivalent to the sum of capabilities independently possessed by each individual.**

Civilization operates through specialization, coordination, accumulated knowledge, and technological infrastructure.

A modern person might be unable to grow food, build a house, manufacture medicine, generate electricity, or construct the communication systems they depend on.

Nevertheless, that person may enjoy a far greater range of capabilities than someone who can independently perform all the basic activities necessary for survival.

Now apply this principle to reasoning.

Imagine a civilization in which machines perform nearly all advanced scientific reasoning, engineering design, economic optimization, and complex intellectual production.

Humans specialize in activities such as choosing objectives, exploring possibilities, experiencing outcomes, and determining which futures they wish to pursue.

Why should that civilization necessarily be considered intellectually diminished?

It could be vastly more capable than ours.

Indeed, **delegating complex reasoning might be a necessary transition for a civilization attempting to solve problems beyond the cognitive capacity of biological individuals.**

We already know that many scientific and engineering problems are too complicated for a single human to solve independently.

A future civilization attempting to manage planetary ecosystems, construct interstellar infrastructure, or understand extremely complicated physical phenomena might encounter problems that overwhelm even large communities of unaided human experts.

AI could make such undertakings possible.

### But specialization eventually encounters a very interesting boundary

There is a difference between our current technological specialization and the hypothetical future we're discussing.

Today, although you cannot independently fabricate a microprocessor, _other humans can_.

There are people who understand the underlying science, people who design the circuits, people who manufacture the equipment, and people who verify the resulting devices.

No individual possesses every piece of knowledge, but the civilization maintains a distributed network of human expertise.

Now imagine that AI systems surpass the combined reasoning capabilities of humanity.

No human fully understands how the most advanced systems work.

No human can independently verify their most complicated conclusions.

Even the systems responsible for checking those conclusions are themselves designed and maintained by other AI systems.

Would that necessarily be a problem?

Not always.

Consider mathematics. An AI might discover a theorem too complex for a human to understand in its entirety, yet provide a formally checkable proof.

We may not understand the reasoning intuitively, but we can verify that it satisfies the rules of a formal system.

For empirical science, AI could design experiments, build instruments, collect observations, and test predictions.

The resulting knowledge could be grounded in physical reality even if no human could independently reproduce every step.

Eventually, AI systems might become the primary intellectual specialists of civilization.

And this suggests an extraordinary possibility:

**A civilization might become increasingly intelligent while the independent reasoning capabilities of its biological population remain unchanged or even decline.**

There is no logical contradiction here.

The relevant unit of intelligence would increasingly be the civilization as a whole.

### How far could such a civilization go?

Potentially much further than a civilization insisting that every important intellectual achievement must remain comprehensible to human minds.

But there is a crucial distinction between technical capability and decision-making authority.

A civilization could possess AI systems capable of extraordinarily advanced reasoning while humans retain meaningful influence over its goals.

Alternatively, the systems could eventually determine not only how to accomplish objectives, but which objectives ought to be pursued.

At that point, AI would no longer be merely a specialized intellectual workforce.

It would have become a major source of civilizational direction.

That outcome might be beneficial, harmful, or something difficult to classify using present-day human categories.

The question becomes whether humans continue to determine what their civilization is attempting to accomplish, and whether such a division of responsibilities remains practical when the systems understand the consequences of decisions much better than the humans making them.

Perhaps even the responsibility for choosing objectives would eventually be delegated.

This would represent a much more profound transformation than simply losing the ability to write books.

## 3. Your author-model idea could fundamentally change what it means to publish a book

I particularly like this direction, because it goes beyond the usual discussion of personalized AI-generated stories.

You're proposing something more specific.

**What if an author's primary creative product becomes a model of their imagination rather than a collection of finished literary works?**

Let's examine that.

Today, an author develops a fictional universe, constructs characters, writes stories, and publishes books.

Each book represents a particular exploration of that author's imagination.

The reader can experience only the specific narrative the author decided to write.

But suppose the author could instead develop a sophisticated creative model.

It would contain their understanding of the fictional universe, their philosophical interests, their storytelling preferences, their imaginative tendencies, and their characteristic ways of developing narratives.

It might also contain an extensive world model capable of generating new situations while preserving the internal logic of the author's fictional creation.

Readers could interact directly with that model.

They could ask it to create stories, explore alternative histories, or investigate events that the author never explicitly described.

The author would no longer have to anticipate every interesting question a reader might ask.

Their creative model could generate responses dynamically.

### The Sherlock Holmes example

Your proposed time-travel train robbery is a great illustration.

There's a useful distinction, though: Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character, while Arthur Conan Doyle was the author.

A future system might offer either a _Holmes personality model_ or a _Conan Doyle creative model_.

Those could produce very different experiences.

Imagine asking a model representing Conan Doyle's literary perspective to construct your story.

It might interpret the concept according to his preferences for mystery construction, narrative pacing, characterization, and deductive reasoning.

A model representing Sherlock Holmes, on the other hand, could treat your scenario as a case to be investigated.

You could accompany Holmes aboard the time-displaced train, ask questions, examine clues, and challenge his deductions.

The system maintains a coherent underlying fictional reality.

When you ask what is happening in another carriage, it must determine an answer consistent with the train's temporal mechanics and everything that has happened so far.

If you ask to rotate the camera and look through a window, the system renders a view consistent with the same fictional world.

If you decide to follow a suspicious passenger rather than accompany Holmes, the story branches.

Holmes continues investigating independently.

Other characters continue behaving according to their motivations.

And the world remains internally consistent.

This is considerably more challenging than simply generating the next paragraph of a story.

The system would need to maintain a persistent world state, coherent causal relationships, character histories, and rules governing the fictional universe.

In your example, the temporal relationships among the carriages would need to be rigorously managed. Otherwise, the mystery could accidentally contradict itself.

But there is no obvious physical principle preventing sufficiently advanced computational systems from implementing many such experiences.

The primary limitations concern computational resources, real-time consistency, world simulation, and the quality of the underlying intelligence.

### The author becomes the creator of a generative intellectual environment

Now imagine that someone spends twenty years refining their creative model.

They contribute new ideas, adjust its storytelling preferences, develop fictional civilizations, and shape its understanding of human behaviour.

They test it repeatedly against their own judgments.

Eventually, the model becomes capable of generating stories that the author themselves finds surprisingly compelling.

The model produces narratives the author never explicitly imagined, while remaining consistent with the intellectual and artistic direction they established.

What has the author created?

Something resembling a **generative literary identity**.

Readers wouldn't purchase individual books. They might subscribe to, license, or otherwise access an author's creative model.

The economic unit of literature could shift from the finished manuscript to the intellectual architecture capable of producing manuscripts.

A traditional book would become one possible output of that architecture.

And if the author died, the model could potentially continue producing new stories for centuries.

Of course, a model trained on someone's writing would not automatically possess that person's mind, intentions, or subjective experiences. That distinction is already being examined in research on personalized AI systems and digital twins. [Springer Link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-026-02875-4?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But if the model faithfully represented a sufficiently rich creative perspective, readers might regard its outputs as meaningful extensions of the author's work.

The author could even maintain direct control over which extensions are considered canonical.

### And yes, Netflix should probably be paying attention!

The interesting disruption isn't merely AI-generated video.

It is the potential disappearance of the distinction between reading a book, watching a film, and participating in a fictional world.

Imagine a fictional universe that can be experienced through text, voice, cinematic video, virtual reality, or direct interaction.

All modalities access the same underlying world model.

You could begin by reading a chapter, switch to a cinematic view, ask a character a question, and then return to reading.

The narrative remains coherent.

Perhaps a movie becomes nothing more than one particular recorded journey through that universe.

A book becomes another.

And a video game becomes yet another.

What happens to publishing companies, film studios, streaming platforms, and gaming companies when these different creative products become interfaces to the same underlying generative systems?

That question may deserve its own Field Readings article.

One important complication is that readers may not always want unlimited freedom. A carefully constructed mystery depends partly on the author controlling what the audience discovers and when. A sophisticated creative model might therefore need to preserve narrative constraints even while allowing exploration.

The future could combine authored narrative structures with dynamic experiences, rather than abandoning narrative structure entirely.

## 4. Could humans eventually consume the vast quantities of knowledge that AI produces?

Your observation about today's information abundance is correct.

We already produce far more potentially valuable material than any individual can consume.

It would be a mistake to assume that everything we haven't read is worthless.

There are undoubtedly excellent books, scientific papers, arguments, and creative works that most interested readers will never encounter.

As the volume of AI-generated content increases, this gap could grow enormously.

But I think we should distinguish between two ambitions.

One is to allow humans to access and make use of vastly more knowledge.

The other is to allow humans to _personally comprehend and retain_ all that knowledge.

These may have very different technological limits.

### First possibility: AI as an extension of human working memory

Imagine having a reliable external cognitive system that maintains everything you're currently investigating.

It remembers every assumption, argument, contradiction, and potentially useful connection.

You can access any part of this intellectual structure instantly.

Instead of repeatedly reconstructing the context of a problem, you simply move your attention to whichever component you wish to examine.

In this arrangement, the AI acts as a kind of external working memory.

This could substantially expand the complexity of problems a person can work on.

And importantly, it doesn't require the person's biological brain to store all the underlying information.

The human remains involved in navigating and developing the intellectual structure.

### Second possibility: Direct neural augmentation

Now imagine that the external system can communicate with the brain through a high-bandwidth neural interface.

Rather than reading an explanation, you might receive an appropriately encoded conceptual representation.

Perhaps a complicated mathematical relationship could be experienced through a new form of spatial intuition.

Perhaps hundreds of relationships could be presented as a structured cognitive object that the brain learns to manipulate.

There is already research into brain-computer interfaces that monitor or influence cognitive states, including attention and memory. A 2026 review describes cognitive interfaces as an emerging field, although today's technology remains far from general-purpose intellectual augmentation. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1364661326001397?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

A separate 2026 study reported improvements in working-memory performance using personalized, adaptive neuromodulation. That is an interesting early result, but it is not evidence that we can upload knowledge or vastly expand human comprehension. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13336854/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The speculative possibility is nevertheless worth exploring.

What if future humans can expand their cognitive capabilities through neural implants, artificial memory systems, or increasingly integrated external computation?

We might eventually develop forms of augmented cognition that make today's reading and learning processes seem extraordinarily inefficient.

### But there is a deeper limit

Suppose a million books contain a million genuinely distinct ideas.

Can those ideas be compressed into a single experience without losing anything?

Not necessarily.

Some information is compressible because it contains repetition, redundancy, or predictable relationships.

But genuinely independent information cannot be compressed arbitrarily without loss.

And understanding isn't merely a matter of transferring information.

A person may need to connect new concepts with existing knowledge, investigate their implications, and develop the mental structures necessary to use them.

A direct neural interface might accelerate these processes enormously.

It doesn't automatically eliminate their underlying computational requirements.

Even an augmented biological mind would have finite resources.

Meanwhile, advanced AI systems could continue generating new knowledge faster than humans can assimilate it.

**We might never catch up with the total volume of available knowledge, even while becoming vastly more intellectually capable.**

But here's an alternative.

Maybe we won't need to.

Instead of learning every scientific paper, we could interact with a system that has synthesized the entire body of relevant knowledge into a navigable conceptual structure.

We could ask it to reveal relationships, test hypotheses, and explore implications.

The important capability would no longer be knowing everything.

It would be the ability to meaningfully investigate anything.

And this connects directly with your first point.

Perhaps the future of intelligence involves progressively replacing the personal possession of knowledge with increasingly sophisticated access to knowledge and reasoning.

## 5. Your observation about the mental effort of creating an idea is important

You mentioned something I think deserves particular attention.

You experience a different kind of cognitive effort when constructing an idea than when merely reading or watching someone else's explanation.

That observation is consistent with an important distinction in cognitive psychology.

Recognizing or following an explanation doesn't necessarily require the same mental operations as independently constructing it.

When reading an argument, many of its relationships have already been organized for you.

When creating the argument, you must identify those relationships yourself.

You may need to retrieve information, organize evidence, formulate intermediate conclusions, recognize contradictions, and maintain a coherent mental representation of the overall reasoning.

These activities involve overlapping but differently recruited cognitive networks. There isn't a single isolated brain region responsible for all complex thought construction.

But the functional distinction you're describing is meaningful.

### What happens if the constructive process is repeatedly delegated?

Imagine a person who regularly receives highly polished explanations from AI.

They become extremely familiar with sophisticated intellectual arguments.

They can recognize terminology, follow explanations, and discuss conclusions.

However, they rarely practice constructing comparable arguments independently.

Their ability to evaluate a well-presented explanation might remain relatively strong.

But their ability to formulate an argument from an unfamiliar starting point could develop less fully.

There is some empirical reason to investigate this possibility.

A 2025 Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon study surveyed 319 knowledge workers and found that greater confidence in generative AI was associated with lower self-reported critical-thinking effort. Importantly, the researchers cautioned that reduced effort could reflect genuine assistance, delegation, or other changes in how people approach tasks. It does not demonstrate that AI has damaged their reasoning abilities. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

What interests me is what might happen over several generations.

If people increasingly receive completed intellectual structures before they have attempted to build their own, could they become very good at recognizing sophisticated ideas while becoming less capable of generating them?

It's plausible.

But your earlier point suggests an equally interesting counterpossibility.

What if those people become better at something else?

Perhaps their intellectual development shifts away from detailed argument construction toward identifying novel relationships among large conceptual structures.

They might become weaker at constructing long deductive chains independently while becoming substantially stronger at exploring interdisciplinary possibilities.

A future human might be unable to independently prove a difficult mathematical theorem but extraordinarily capable of imagining productive connections between mathematical theories, physical phenomena, and biological systems.

Would that person be less intelligent?

I don't think the answer is obvious.

### The problem of detecting incorrect reasoning

However, there's an issue worth pursuing.

Suppose someone becomes accustomed to evaluating the conclusions of sophisticated AI-generated arguments without constructing the arguments themselves.

Eventually, AI develops an incorrect but extremely persuasive argument.

Can the person recognize the error?

Perhaps.

They might have retained enough understanding of the underlying principles to identify inconsistencies.

But what if the mistake exists deep inside a chain of reasoning that the person cannot independently reconstruct?

Then the person may have to rely on another AI system to evaluate it.

And another system to verify that evaluation.

This could become a recursive chain of machine-mediated verification.

That isn't automatically a failure. We already use specialized instruments and software to verify results we cannot evaluate unaided.

But it raises a significant question:

**How does a civilization maintain reliable intellectual oversight once its most sophisticated reasoning processes exceed the independent comprehension of its members?**

The answer might involve mathematical verification, empirical testing, diverse independent AI systems, or new forms of human-machine cognition.

It might also involve accepting that humans no longer exercise comprehensive intellectual oversight.

Again, that would be a major civilizational transition rather than necessarily a technological failure.

## 6. Does AI-generated writing eventually need to be grounded in human understanding?

Your final observation is particularly interesting because it identifies a potential limitation on fully automated literary production.

You mentioned the _Library of Babel_, presumably referring to Borges's imagined library containing every possible arrangement of characters.

The important point is that producing text doesn't guarantee producing meaning.

A system could generate an enormous quantity of internally consistent material that humans find incomprehensible, irrelevant, or emotionally uninteresting.

And I think you're correct that future writing systems would need mechanisms to ensure their outputs remain meaningful to their intended audiences.

However, I would distinguish **grounding** from **human authorship**.

### Machines can evaluate some properties of writing independently

Suppose an AI writes a complicated mystery involving temporal paradoxes.

Other AI systems could verify that the plot obeys its established rules.

They could check whether characters possess information they shouldn't know.

They could evaluate chronological consistency.

They could determine whether a solution follows logically from previously established clues.

These tasks could potentially be automated to a high degree.

There is no inherent requirement for human writers to perform them.

But whether a reader finds the mystery satisfying is a different question.

Does the reader understand the clues?

Are the characters emotionally convincing?

Is the explanation confusing?

Does the narrative create suspense?

Does the ending feel satisfying rather than arbitrary?

These are properties involving the reader's experience.

### Could AI evaluate the human experience without involving humans?

Here's where your question becomes especially interesting.

Initially, AI systems might need human readers to evaluate their output.

Readers could provide feedback, ratings, comments, and behavioural signals.

Systems would learn which characteristics make stories comprehensible, compelling, or emotionally satisfying.

Eventually, AI might develop extremely sophisticated models of human readers.

It could simulate different readers and estimate their responses.

A future writing system might test a novel against millions of simulated reader profiles before publishing it.

But how would it know those simulations remained accurate?

It would need some connection to actual human responses.

That connection could come from reading behaviour, direct feedback, eye tracking, neurological measurements, or other observations.

It wouldn't necessarily require human authors or editors.

**Humans might remain essential as the reference population for human-oriented literature without remaining essential as the producers of that literature.**

This is an important distinction.

And there's already relevant research suggesting that literary evaluation should account for differences between readers. A 2025 ACL study analyzing 1,471 stories and responses from 101 annotators investigated how textual characteristics and individual reader preferences affect assessments of AI-generated creative writing. [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.1304/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### But perhaps even this grounding requirement eventually disappears

Let's think bigger, as you suggested.

Suppose an AI develops a sufficiently accurate computational model of human cognition.

It can predict how different people will respond to a story, including their comprehension, emotional reactions, and aesthetic preferences.

It may no longer require continuous feedback from human readers for every creative decision.

Instead, it uses its models to anticipate human responses.

At that point, the system could produce literature for humans without requiring any human involvement in the writing process.

And perhaps the models become so sophisticated that their predictions are more reliable than the judgments of individual human editors.

Nevertheless, if the objective remains producing experiences that actual humans value, there is still a conceptual distinction between predicting human responses and measuring them.

The system's predictions might be excellent.

But without occasional real-world validation, it could become systematically mistaken about the population it is trying to serve.

This is the difference between an accurate model and a model that merely appears accurate to itself.

### The AI-training feedback loop

There is another related issue.

What happens when AI systems increasingly train on material produced by other AI systems?

Research has demonstrated that particular forms of recursive training on synthetic data can lead to model collapse, with successive generations losing useful properties of the original data distribution. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But this is not a universal prohibition against AI-generated training data.

A 2025 ICML paper found that some training arrangements incorporating synthetic data alongside accumulated real data could avoid the collapse observed when real data was repeatedly replaced. [Proceedings of Machine Learning Research](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/kazdan25a.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The lesson is that recursively generated information needs appropriate evaluation and grounding.

And the grounding source doesn't always have to be human-written material.

For mathematics, it may be formal correctness.

For physics, it may be experimental observation.

For engineering, it may be whether the constructed system actually works.

For human-oriented literature, it may ultimately be human comprehension and experience.

That brings us to a subtle possibility.

### What if machines develop their own literature?

Imagine a civilization in which highly advanced AI systems communicate using representations far more sophisticated than natural human languages.

They might exchange complicated conceptual structures that cannot be easily translated into written English.

What would it mean for such systems to produce literature?

Their creative works could be extraordinarily complex.

They might explore mathematical structures, abstract relationships, simulated experiences, or forms of intelligence that human beings find incomprehensible.

Those works might be perfectly meaningful to the machines.

They would only resemble gibberish from our perspective.

If these systems wanted to communicate their ideas to humans, they would need translation mechanisms.

But the translation might necessarily discard information that humans lack the cognitive capacity to understand.

In that case, the problem would no longer be whether AI possesses adequate writing skills.

The problem would be whether the human mind can comprehend the underlying content at all.

**Human-readable writing might eventually become a simplified interface to a much larger body of machine-generated knowledge and creativity.**

And the role of human writers, if they remained involved, could shift toward identifying which parts of that knowledge are worth communicating and how they should be interpreted.

That is a very different future from merely using AI to generate novels faster.

# 7. There's a larger question emerging from all this

I think your observations are beginning to challenge the premise of our original discussion.

We began by asking whether AI-generated books might make human authors obsolete.

Then we considered whether outsourcing writing could weaken human reasoning.

But now we're confronting something considerably larger:

**Could a civilization become more intelligent precisely because its individual members no longer need to perform the complex intellectual activities that earlier generations considered essential?**

Consider the progression.

Humanity develops writing to preserve and communicate ideas beyond the limits of individual memory.

Libraries and printing dramatically expand access to knowledge.

Computers automate increasingly complicated calculations.

Networks connect people and information across the planet.

AI begins automating reasoning, synthesis, and creative production.

Eventually, human-machine interfaces might allow people to interact with intellectual structures far beyond the capacity of unaided biological cognition.

At each stage, some activities previously performed by individuals are delegated to external systems.

And at each stage, new kinds of intellectual activity become possible.

Perhaps the next transition involves the outsourcing of complex reasoning itself.

### But what would humans actually do?

This is where I think the investigation becomes especially provocative.

Imagine a civilization in which machines can perform virtually every intellectual activity more effectively than humans.

They can conduct scientific research.

They can create extraordinary literature.

They can design technology.

They can develop philosophical arguments.

They can anticipate the consequences of decisions.

They can even generate entirely new intellectual disciplines that humans lack the capacity to understand.

What remains for humans?

One possibility is that humans become primarily experiential beings.

They explore, enjoy, create, form relationships, and pursue personally meaningful activities without needing to understand the complicated systems sustaining their civilization.

Another possibility is that humans augment themselves sufficiently to participate in the expanded intellectual environment.

Their cognitive capabilities evolve alongside those of the machines.

A third possibility is that the distinction between human and machine intelligence gradually dissolves.

The civilization becomes a distributed cognitive system incorporating biological minds, artificial minds, external memory, and computational infrastructure.

In that future, asking whether humans can independently construct complex arguments might become as strange as asking whether a single neuron can independently formulate a philosophical theory.

A neuron cannot.

A human brain can.

Perhaps an individual human couldn't independently develop the most sophisticated ideas of a future civilization.

But the civilization could.

And perhaps that would be sufficient.

Or perhaps individual cognitive independence turns out to possess a value that cannot be fully reproduced through collective intelligence.

We don't yet know.

## One final thought: The difference between intellectual capability and intellectual experience

There is something I think we should preserve as an open question throughout this investigation.

Suppose two future humans have access to the same extraordinarily capable AI system.

The first asks the system to investigate a philosophical problem and explain its conclusions.

The second spends months struggling with the problem independently, occasionally using the AI to test ideas and identify contradictions.

Eventually, both understand the conclusion.

Perhaps the first arrives at it much faster.

Perhaps the first even understands the final argument more thoroughly.

But have they undergone the same intellectual experience?

Probably not.

The second person has experienced uncertainty, confusion, failed hypotheses, and the gradual emergence of understanding.

Those experiences might alter their cognition in ways that simply receiving an explanation does not.

Or perhaps future neurotechnology could reproduce the relevant learning processes directly, without requiring months of conscious struggle.

If so, even that distinction might eventually disappear.

This raises a remarkably difficult question:

**Is the process of thinking valuable because of what it produces, because of what it does to the thinker, or because of the experience of thinking itself?**

These are three different possibilities.

If thinking matters primarily for its results, AI delegation may be overwhelmingly beneficial.

If thinking matters because it develops cognitive capabilities, we need to investigate whether future technologies can develop those capabilities more effectively.

If thinking itself is a valued experience, people may continue pursuing difficult intellectual activities even when machines can perform them effortlessly.

And none of these outcomes requires preserving traditional writing for everyone.

## 1. The author as a tour guide through a simulated universe

The author identifies the interesting ones, refines them, and establishes paths that future visitors can follow.

But there's an important distinction between a traditional book and what you're proposing.

**The author is creating an environment in which stories can unfold, while retaining the ability to guide visitors toward particularly interesting experiences.**

A conventional novel contains a particular sequence of events selected by its author.

Your proposed system contains a space of possible events, together with mechanisms for guiding the visitor through it.

### The narrative path becomes a creative artifact

The author identifies these possibilities and constructs an interesting mystery around them.

However, instead of presenting the resulting story as a fixed sequence of chapters, the author creates a _guided route_ through the simulated world.

A passenger might mention something unusual.

An object might catch the visitor's attention.

A character might deliberately avoid answering a question.

Each of these elements gently encourages the visitor to investigate a particular aspect of the universe.

The author is effectively designing the circumstances under which discoveries occur.

An AI could potentially discover which perceptual tasks each individual performs particularly well.

That's a strange form of expertise, isn't it?

Someone could become extraordinarily valuable to an advanced scientific system because of how particular mathematical objects _feel_ to them.

---

## 4. The really interesting possibility: AI might discover that human diversity is computationally useful

There's another dimension here.

We have been talking about human visual perception as though all humans perceive artistic structures in roughly the same way.

But aesthetic preferences vary considerably.

What one person finds beautiful, another might find unpleasant.

What one person regards as chaotic, another might experience as harmonious.

Those differences are often treated as obstacles to objective aesthetic evaluation.

**What if the differences themselves become valuable?**

Imagine an AI attempting to organize an immense body of information.

It develops a variety of visual projections.

Different groups of humans respond differently to those projections.

The system discovers that these variations reflect distinct ways of organizing perceived relationships.

Perhaps one group consistently recognizes structural regularities that another overlooks.

Another group is exceptionally sensitive to unusual combinations.

Still another responds strongly to patterns that lie near the boundaries of familiar categories.

The AI could use these responses to construct several overlapping classifications of the same information.

Instead of seeking one universal human aesthetic judgment, it exploits the diversity of human perception.

This could become especially useful when the underlying information admits multiple valid forms of organization.

Remember our earlier discussion of the future _knowledge navigator_?

We imagined experts who understand particular regions of an enormous intellectual landscape.

Now imagine that some of those experts navigate the landscape through sensory intuition rather than explicit conceptual knowledge.

A scientist might consult someone whose perceptual abilities are unusually effective at recognizing certain classes of relationships.

The expert examines a visualization and says:

"These two structures feel related. I can't explain exactly why, but there's something similar about them."

The AI investigates that intuition and discovers a previously overlooked relationship.

Perhaps this becomes a recognized kind of human-machine research collaboration.

And if those perceptual abilities can be trained, we might develop educational disciplines concerned with expanding the kinds of structures humans can recognize.

Aesthetic training could acquire an entirely new scientific purpose.

---

## 5. But wouldn't an advanced AI simply simulate human perception?

This is the most important technical objection.

And I don't think we should dismiss it.

If AI becomes sufficiently advanced, why would it need biological humans to perform these evaluations?

It could build computational models of human perception.

It could simulate millions of different aesthetic preferences.

It could run those evaluations much faster than real humans.

This could eliminate the computational advantage of involving biological observers.

Indeed, human participation introduces costs: communication, limited attention, inconsistent responses, and relatively slow interaction.

The fact that the human brain operates on roughly tens of watts doesn't automatically mean a human-assisted computation is cheaper or more effective than an artificial one.

However, several possibilities remain.

**First, biological perception might occasionally supply an independent computational perspective.**

Even if AI systems can model human perception extremely well, actual humans might still generate unexpected responses.

Those responses could reveal weaknesses in the models or relationships the systems had overlooked.

**Second, human perception might be useful precisely because of its unusual imperfections.**

Our sensory systems have evolved under particular biological and environmental constraints.

They contain biases, sensitivities, and limitations that differ from those of artificial systems.

Sometimes these peculiarities might produce useful shortcuts.

A machine could deliberately exploit them, rather than attempting to reproduce an idealized version of perception.

**Third, human aesthetic responses could provide grounding.**

If the system's objective involves human experience, actual human judgments provide evidence that simulations alone cannot fully guarantee.

But there's an even more speculative fourth possibility.

What if AI discovers that certain biological computational processes are extraordinarily efficient for particular tasks?

In that case, it could conceivably use biological neural systems as specialized computational components.

Those systems might be biological organisms, cultivated neural tissue, or hybrid biological-electronic devices.

Human participants would be one possible implementation, although not necessarily the most practical.

This would transform the idea of _biological computing_ from a metaphor into something more literal.

We should distinguish this speculative possibility from current research. There is no established evidence that human aesthetic responses provide a broadly superior way of solving arbitrary mathematical or scientific optimization problems.

But the conditions under which they _could_ become useful are worth investigating.

---

## 6. What if the AI-generated artwork has two entirely different meanings?

I want to return to your original thought about novel artistic styles.

Suppose an AI produces a visual composition.

A human sees an extraordinary abstract painting.

They experience something unfamiliar: a strange mixture of beauty, tension, anticipation, and pleasure.

They might spend hours examining the work.

But the AI perceives something else.

Perhaps the composition represents a complicated mathematical relationship.

Perhaps its structure encodes a region of an enormous scientific knowledge space.

Perhaps the relationships among its colours, shapes, and movements correspond to features of a complex optimization problem.

For the AI, the artwork has informational significance.

For the human, it has aesthetic significance.

**The same object might simultaneously function as a machine-readable intellectual structure and a human-experienced artistic creation.**

And now consider what happens if humans begin developing preferences for these unfamiliar forms.

They create names for them.

They establish artistic movements around them.

They begin recognizing increasingly subtle differences.

Eventually, people become extraordinarily skilled at distinguishing varieties of machine-generated structures.

The AI could potentially use this emerging human expertise to construct new classification systems.

An artistic culture might develop around something that also serves as part of a civilization's information-processing infrastructure.

That would be quite an outcome.

People might spend their evenings appreciating beautiful visual compositions while their aesthetic judgments help organize enormous bodies of scientific knowledge.

The activity would be simultaneously cultural and computational.

And perhaps nobody would find this strange.

After all, we already perform computational work when we interact with digital systems, provide ratings, categorize images, or make selections that help train recommendation algorithms.

Your proposed future would make the relationship much more sophisticated.

---

## 7. Could aesthetic experience become an interface to knowledge beyond human comprehension?

This takes us back to your idea of humanity struggling to navigate an ever-expanding body of knowledge.

Suppose scientific knowledge becomes so complicated that humans cannot understand much of it through natural language.

A conventional explanation might require thousands of pages of unfamiliar mathematics.

But an advanced AI might discover ways to represent certain structural properties through sensory experiences.

For example, a complicated mathematical transformation might be encoded as a changing visual environment.

The person learns to recognize particular relationships through motion and spatial structure.

They might eventually develop an intuition for the transformation without being able to articulate its complete mathematics.

This wouldn't mean they possess the full underlying knowledge.

It would mean they have acquired a useful perceptual representation of some of its properties.

We already do something conceptually related when using graphs to recognize trends that would be difficult to identify by examining thousands of numerical values.

Future systems could extend this principle far beyond conventional visualization.

Perhaps entirely new visual aesthetics would arise from attempts to make extraordinarily complicated intellectual structures perceptually navigable.

And perhaps human experts would specialize in different parts of this sensory landscape.

Someone might be unusually skilled at navigating representations of complex dynamical systems.

Another might recognize patterns associated with particular mathematical structures.

Another might develop an intuitive understanding of relationships that the AI itself represents through entirely different computational mechanisms.

The crucial limitation is that a sensory representation cannot automatically convey unlimited information. Human perception has finite capacity, and a useful abstraction necessarily selects or compresses aspects of the underlying structure.

Nevertheless, **a person need not understand every detail of an intellectual object to navigate certain meaningful relationships within it**.

Which brings us directly back to your earlier observations about learning mathematics.

You enjoy discovering what mathematical concepts make possible, even when you haven't mastered every procedural detail.

Perhaps future cognitive interfaces will allow humans to explore increasingly abstract intellectual spaces in precisely this fashion.

And perhaps aesthetic experience will become one of the mechanisms that makes such exploration possible.

---

## 8. There's an extraordinary possibility hidden in your computational-shortcut idea

We've been assuming that the AI knows what it's looking for and uses human perception to help find it.

But what if the AI doesn't know?

Imagine an artificial intelligence investigating an intellectual domain so unfamiliar that it lacks an effective way of determining which structures are interesting.

It can generate possibilities.

It can identify mathematical relationships.

It can perform enormous quantities of computation.

But it doesn't yet know which relationships deserve attention.

Now suppose it begins presenting different representations to humans.

Some provoke indifference.

Others produce unusually strong aesthetic responses.

The AI investigates the structures associated with those responses.

Occasionally, it discovers something unexpected.

Over time, it learns that certain biological perceptual reactions are useful for identifying regions of possibility space worth exploring.

The humans are effectively contributing an additional heuristic for novelty or significance.

This is especially interesting because **aesthetic judgment can operate before a person knows why something is interesting**.

Of course, aesthetic interest isn't a reliable indicator of scientific importance. A compelling pattern can be meaningless, and a scientifically profound result can look visually ordinary.

The system would need to establish genuine predictive relationships through independent evaluation.

But suppose it succeeds in some domains.

Then human aesthetic perception might become one of several tools used by AI to identify promising intellectual directions.

And the machines might deliberately develop artistic experiences to probe those perceptual capabilities.

In that scenario, AI-generated art would serve three purposes simultaneously:

1. Providing experiences humans find interesting or enjoyable.
2. Helping humans navigate complicated intellectual structures.
3. Allowing machines to extract useful information from biological perceptual responses.

The relationship between art, science, and computation would become unusually intimate.

---

## The implication I find most striking

We started this discussion by wondering whether AI-generated literature might render human creativity obsolete.

But your idea suggests a rather different possibility.

**What if human creativity, aesthetic taste, and sensory experience become valuable components of a much larger intelligence precisely because they operate differently from artificial cognition?**

It wouldn't require humans to be more intelligent than machines.

It wouldn't require human artists to produce better work than AI.

It wouldn't even require humans to understand the intellectual structures they were helping explore.

Their biological perceptual systems could contribute distinctive evaluations to a larger computational process.

And those evaluations might themselves evolve as humans encounter increasingly unfamiliar machine-generated artistic forms.

There is a curious circularity here.

AI creates new aesthetic experiences.

Humans develop the ability to appreciate and distinguish them.

AI discovers useful information in those distinctions.

It creates increasingly sophisticated aesthetic structures.

Human perceptual expertise develops further.

Over generations, entirely new forms of human-machine intellectual collaboration might emerge.

Of course, AI could eventually simulate or replace every useful component of this arrangement. That remains a genuine possibility.

But there's no reason to assume that such replacement must occur before hybrid systems have developed entirely new cognitive practices.

### A question for Field Readings

This opens up an article quite different from the one we originally contemplated:

**"Could Human Taste Become a Computing Resource for Artificial Intelligence?"**

And perhaps an even stranger version:

**"What If the Art We Enjoy Is Actually Helping Machines Think?"**

The interesting possibility is that humans could remain important to an extraordinarily advanced machine civilization through capabilities we currently regard as subjective, recreational, or even irrational.

I would take this one step further, though.

Imagine that an AI discovers a particular visual pattern that reliably produces an unusual response in the human brain. The person experiences it as an extraordinary new form of beauty. The machine discovers that the pattern's effect allows it to perform some useful classification or optimization.

**To the human, it is art. To the machine, it is an algorithm.**

And perhaps both interpretations would be equally valid.

That is a future worth exploring.
